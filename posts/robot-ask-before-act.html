A Robot Should Sometimes Ask Before It Acts

Clarification is not merely conversation. In interactive robotics, it can be an information-gathering control action with a measurable cost and value.

September 10, 2026•6 min read•Md Shariful Islam

Imagine a person says, “bring me that bottle,” while two bottles are plausible targets. A robot can guess, wait, look again, or ask a question. Which behavior is best depends on more than classifier confidence.

Asking has a cost

Clarification interrupts the user. Repeated questions make an assistant slow and annoying. In some settings, speech may not even be convenient. So “ask whenever uncertain” is not a strong policy.

But acting also has a cost. A wrong pick may waste time, spill something, violate a user preference, or make recovery difficult. The decision should compare these costs.

Clarification as active information acquisition

An effective question should split the current hypotheses. “Do you mean the bottle?” is weak if both candidates are bottles. “Do you mean the blue bottle near the laptop?” may resolve the ambiguity immediately.

Design rule: ask the smallest question that is expected to change the action decision.

What HCI adds

Pure decision theory can optimize expected task cost, but human interaction adds factors such as interruption timing, cognitive load, predictability, user preference, and trust calibration. A technically optimal number of questions may still feel poor to a user if they arrive at the wrong moment.

An experiment I would trust

Compare at least four policies: no clarification, fixed uncertainty threshold, information-gain clarification, and a user-cost-aware policy. Use ambiguous tasks with controlled difficulty. Report not only success, but also unnecessary questions, wrong commitments, completion time, correction effort, and subjective interruption burden.

The core HCI question is whether the robot’s uncertainty behavior becomes legible. Users should be able to form a reasonable expectation of when the robot will proceed and when it will ask.

Note: This is a research note, not a peer-reviewed claim. It is meant to make a technical question precise enough to test.

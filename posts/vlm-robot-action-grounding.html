What VLMs Still Need Before They Can Reliably Drive Robot Actions

Visual-language understanding is impressive, but robotics requires a stricter contract: state grounding, temporal consistency, uncertainty, and consequences.

September 14, 2026•8 min read•Md Shariful Islam

Vision-language models can describe complex images, answer questions, and follow high-level instructions. That does not automatically make them reliable robot policies. A robot needs a representation that remains useful when the camera moves, objects are occluded, the scene changes, and an action has physical consequences.

Recognition is not action grounding

A VLM might correctly answer “the red cup is left of the bowl.” A manipulation system needs much more: which red cup, in what coordinate frame, with what pose, whether it is reachable, whether the relation is still true after the previous action, and what uncertainty surrounds the estimate.

The gap is therefore not only semantic. It is geometric, temporal, and causal.

Four failure modes I would test first

  • Identity drift: does the model keep referring to the same object across viewpoint changes?
  • State staleness: does it continue reasoning from a scene fact that became false after an action?
  • Hidden uncertainty: does fluent language mask weak visual evidence?
  • Action underspecification: can a high-level instruction be converted into constraints that a controller can verify?
Evaluation principle: do not score only whether the model says the right thing. Score whether its representation remains correct enough to support the next physical decision.

A useful architecture boundary

I prefer separating semantic proposal from execution authority. A VLM can propose objects, relations, goals, or action sketches. A downstream verifier can check geometry, collision constraints, confidence, state consistency, and recoverability before execution.

This does not reduce the importance of the VLM. It gives the VLM a role that can be evaluated. It also makes failures diagnosable: perception error, grounding error, planning error, or control error.

Beyond single-image benchmarks

For robotics, evaluation should include controlled interventions. Move one object, change one attribute, occlude a target, or revise an instruction. Then test whether the model updates only the beliefs that should change. This exposes whether the representation is actually grounded or simply generating a plausible continuation.

What would make this research convincing?

A strong study should compare multiple VLMs under the same perception and control interface, include a non-VLM baseline, report uncertainty and failure categories, and separate open-loop reasoning from closed-loop execution. The most useful metric may not be final success alone; it may be how often the system notices that it should not yet act.

Note: This is a research note, not a peer-reviewed claim. It is meant to make a technical question precise enough to test.
